[mansourian, 2025]

this paper is a big classification of the current situation in KD practices.
it sorts methods along five independent axes, sections 2-6. one method can
sit on several axes at once.

section 2 focuses on what is transferred to student from teacher:
> logit based
    logits are the outputs of the last layer. softmax over them gives soft
    labels, one probability per class. the non-argmax probabilities carry
    "dark knowledge", meaning which classes look alike. temperature softens
    the softmax so these small values show up; KL divergence measures how
    far the student's distribution is from the teacher's. hinton 2015 is
    here.
> feature based
    the student copies the teacher's intermediate, hidden layer outputs,
    not just the final answer. started with fitnets "hints". since teacher
    and student layers often differ in size, a transformation function maps
    one onto the other before comparing. attention transfer also starts here.
> similarity based
    transfers relations instead of values: how features, channels, samples
    or classes relate to each other inside the teacher, as pairwise
    similarity. the student must keep the same structure, not the same
    numbers. example: FSP, the inner product between two layers.

section 3 splits by when the teacher is trained:
> offline
    two stages: teacher is pre-trained, then frozen while the student
    learns. the most common scheme. downsides: loading the teacher costs
    memory, the student is capped by the teacher, teacher bias is copied.
    the only option when the teacher is behind an API, as with LLMs.
> online
    one stage: teacher and student or several students train together.
    often there is no big pre-trained teacher at all; a group of students
    or an ensemble of branches acts as a "virtual teacher", also called
    mutual learning.
    note: online is not the same as on-policy, see song & zheng 2026.
> self-distillation
    teacher and student are the same network. born-again networks,
    furlanello 2018: a fresh copy learns from the trained copy. also:
    deeper layers teaching shallower ones, earlier epochs teaching later
    ones. shown to be theoretically linked to label smoothing.

section 4 splits by which technique does the transfer:
> attention based
    transfers attention maps, which show where the teacher "looks" in the
    input, so the student focuses on the same important regions. CAM and
    grad-CAM can be used as attention sources.
> adversarial
    borrows the GAN min-max game. three uses. first, data-free KD: a
    generator makes fake training data when the real data is unavailable.
    second, a discriminator tries to tell teacher and student outputs apart
    and the student learns to fool it. third, adversarial robust
    distillation: the student matches the teacher on attacked, perturbed
    inputs.
> multi-teacher
    several teachers, knowledge combined by averaging logits, voting or
    picking one at random. hard part: aggregating well, since each teacher
    has its own capacity gap to the student.
> cross-modal
    a well-labeled modality teaches a poorly-labeled one, for example lidar
    to rgb. the extra modality is used only in training and can be missing
    at inference.
> graph based
    features or samples become graph vertices, their relations become edges,
    and this structure is transferred. captures higher-order relations.
    also covers distilling GNNs themselves.
> adaptive
    distillation settings change during training instead of staying fixed:
    adaptive loss or weights, sample importance, ignoring wrong teacher
    outputs, lowering teacher influence near the end. teacher assistant,
    used against the capacity gap, fits here.
> contrastive
    uses positives and negatives: the student's feature, the anchor, should
    be close to the teacher's feature for the same input, the positive, and
    far from features of other inputs, the negatives.

section 5 splits by the type of input data. mostly task lists, few new
concepts:
> 3d input
    point clouds, voxels, lidar. problems: sparsity and occlusion. tasks:
    3d detection, segmentation, shape classification, depth, completion.
> multi-view input
    several camera views of one scene. typical case: lidar + camera fusion
    teacher, camera-only student.
> text input
    classic NLP tasks like translation, QA, generation and summarization,
    with BERT-sized models compressed.
> speech input
    speech recognition, enhancement, speaker verification. strong focus on
    real-time and embedded deployment.
> video input
    action recognition, video object segmentation. extra concern: keeping
    the output consistent across frames.

section 6 splits by application area:
> large language models
    split into white-box, where teacher logits and hidden states are
    accessible, and black-box, where only the teacher's text is
    accessible. white-box: BERT-based and decoder-based LLMs; MiniLLM uses
    reverse KL; GKD trains on the student's own outputs, mentioned in one
    sentence only, with no on-policy discussion. black-box: in-context
    learning, chain of thought, instruction following. also pruning +
    distillation in industry, such as nvidia minitron.
> foundation models
    big pre-trained models reused for many tasks: CLIP, SAM, DINO, vision
    transformers. used for open-vocabulary and zero-shot tasks.
> self-supervised learning
    no labels. the teacher is often a moving average, EMA, of the student,
    as in BYOL and DINO; two augmentations of one image should give similar
    embeddings.
> diffusion models
    goal is fewer denoising steps at inference. merge several steps into
    one, or train consistency models that generate in a single step.
> visual recognition
    mostly a summary table of task x source x scheme: detection,
    segmentation, pose, face recognition, medical imaging and more.

[deepseek-ai, 2025]

this paper is mainly about training a reasoning model with RL. distillation
is a side part: R1 is used as a teacher to make small dense models good at
reasoning. notes follow arXiv v2, jan 2026, where distillation is spread
over appendix B.3.3, B.4.3 and F.

where it sits in KD terms:
> scheme
    offline. the teacher R1 is trained first and then only used to
    produce data; it is never updated together with the students.
> source
    black-box, sequence-level. no logits or hidden states are used, only
    the text R1 writes. the paper calls the students "distilled", but in
    mansourian's terms this is chain of thought distillation.
> policy
    off-policy. students only imitate teacher-written answers; they never
    get feedback on their own outputs. see song & zheng 2026 for why this
    matters: exposure bias.
> training method
    plain SFT, supervised fine-tuning. no RL stage for the students; the
    authors say adding RL could boost them further but leave it to others.

appendix B.3.3 is the data, what the students learn from:
> reasoning data
    about 600k samples. made by rejection sampling: several answers are
    sampled from an R1 checkpoint for each prompt and only the correct ones
    are kept. correctness checked by rules or by DeepSeek-V3 acting as a
    judge. chains of thought with mixed languages, very long paragraphs or
    code blocks are thrown out.
> non-reasoning data
    about 200k samples: writing, factual QA, translation, self-cognition.
    partly reused from DeepSeek-V3's SFT data. simple queries like
    greetings get no chain of thought.
> dataset as a whole
    about 800k samples, roughly half math and a quarter code. average
    length about 5,355 tokens, mostly single-turn. human annotators checked
    the generated reasoning.

appendix B.4.3 is how the students are trained:
> students
    six open models used as starting points: qwen2.5-math 1.5B and 7B,
    qwen2.5 14B and 32B, llama-3.1 8B, llama-3.3 70B instruct.
> settings
    fine-tuned on the 800k set for 2-3 epochs, cosine learning rate decay,
    max context 32,768 tokens, batch size 64. smaller students get a higher
    learning rate.

appendix F is the results:
> distilled vs human data
    students trained on teacher outputs beat students trained directly on
    human-written data. the paper links this to busbridge et al. 2025,
    distillation scaling laws.
> size effect
    even the 1.5B student beats non-reasoning baselines like GPT-4o on math
    benchmarks. performance rises steadily with student size.
    example, AIME 2024 pass@1: GPT-4o 9.3, distill-qwen-7B 55.5,
    distill-qwen-32B 72.6.
> distillation vs RL
    on small models, distilling from R1 works better than running large-scale
    RL directly on the small model. main takeaway: for a small model it is
    cheaper and better to learn from a strong teacher than to discover
    reasoning on its own.

open points worth remembering:
> what is not tested
    no logit-based or on-policy variant is tried, so it is unknown how much
    is lost by using only text.
> link to my experiment
    the whole recipe is just "generate, filter, SFT", so it can be shrunk:
    teacher text from an API or an open dataset, a small student, LoRA.

[song & zheng, 2026]

this paper is a survey of only one corner of KD: on-policy distillation for
LLMs. its main claim is that off-policy and on-policy are two different
paradigms, not two variants of the same thing, and that earlier surveys
missed this. arXiv preprint, april 2026, tencent.

how it relates to the other two papers:
> vs mansourian
    mansourian maps all of KD; this paper zooms into one corner of that map.
    its "teacher access" axis is the same as mansourian's white-box /
    black-box split, but here it lives one level below the on/off-policy
    split.
> vs deepseek
    R1 distillation is used as the main example of off-policy done well,
    and the paper spends a section explaining why it works anyway.

section 2 gives the background and the core problem:
> token-level vs sequence-level
    token-level KD applies hinton's idea to text: at every position, match
    the teacher's next-token distribution. it assumes the text so far is
    error-free. sequence-level KD, kim & rush, matches whole outputs
    instead; in practice this means training on the teacher's best output.
> exposure bias
    the core problem. in off-policy training the student only sees text
    prefixes from the dataset or teacher; at test time it sees its own
    prefixes, mistakes included. it never got any signal for those states,
    so errors compound. framed with imitation learning: DAgger, ross et al.
    shows the error grows with the square of the length off-policy, but
    only linearly when training on your own states.
> f-divergences
    the family of losses used to compare teacher and student. forward KL is
    mode-covering: the student spreads mass over everything the teacher
    might say, which for a small student gives vague, averaged text.
    reverse KL is mode-seeking: the student commits to the teacher's main
    answers and ignores the rest. JSD sits in between.
> unified view
    every modern on-policy method is one formula with two choices: whose
    text is sampled, and which divergence is used. GKD mixes dataset and
    student text with a ratio lambda; lambda 1 is fully on-policy, lambda 0
    is classic off-policy KD. MiniLLM uses reverse KL on student text.

section 3 is the taxonomy, three orthogonal axes inside on-policy KD:
> feedback signal
    what the teacher gives back. logit-based: a full distribution at every
    token, the densest signal. outcome-based: one score for the whole
    answer, from a reward model or verifier, closer to RL. self-play: the
    model judges itself.
> teacher access
    white-box: teacher weights available, exact divergence possible, but
    the teacher must sit in memory next to the student. black-box: API
    only, so the teacher can only score or rank student outputs.
    teacher-free: no stronger teacher, the model teaches itself.
> granularity
    when the loss is applied. token-level: every step, stable but
    short-sighted. sequence-level: only at the end, sparse, hard to tell
    which step was wrong. hybrid: per reasoning step, e.g. process reward
    models.

section 4 covers white-box on-policy methods:
> divergence choice
    progression from one fixed divergence to adaptive ones that change per
    token or over training. GKD defaults to JSD.
> token weighting
    not all tokens matter equally; reasoning steps count more than
    punctuation, so some methods reweight or select tokens.
> cross-architecture
    standard white-box KD needs the same vocabulary for teacher and student.
    projector-based methods map one model's space onto the other's.
> capacity gap
    above roughly a 10x size ratio, the student blurs the teacher's modes
    together. fix: move the target slowly from the student's own
    distribution toward the teacher's during training.

section 5 covers black-box and self-distillation:
> black-box on-policy
    student writes, teacher can only judge. GAD: a discriminator learns to
    tell student text from teacher text, and its score is the reward.
    lion: teacher finds where the student is weak and writes harder
    prompts.
> self-play
    the model competes with its earlier versions, e.g. SPIN. saturates fast
    because no new information enters the loop.
> privileged information
    the way out of saturation: the same model is teacher when it sees the
    true answer and student when it does not. OPSD does this for math and
    matches GRPO at 4B and 8B with far fewer tokens, but not at 1.7B.

section 6 is reasoning distillation:
> chain-of-thought distillation
    training students on teacher reasoning traces, the black-box route.
> deepseek-R1
    named as strictly off-policy: about 800k static samples, plain SFT, no
    student rollouts, no logits. three reasons it still works: the data is
    very high quality, the long traces already contain self-correction
    like "wait, let me reconsider", and math has few valid solution paths
    to cover. still, the off-policy ceiling is real: later work adds
    on-policy steps after R1-style SFT and gains more.

section 7 is industry and cost:
> deployment
    qwen3 uses on-policy distillation for its small models, with teacher
    signals possibly combined from several experts. gemma 2 uses
    distillation during pre-training.
> compute trade-off
    on-policy costs several times more per token, because the student must
    generate text every step. advice: off-policy is enough for general
    knowledge and style; for reasoning, code and math, the extra cost pays
    off. recommended recipe: off-policy warm-up first, then on-policy.

section 8 lists open problems:
> scaling laws
    busbridge et al. 2025 did this for off-policy; nothing yet for
    on-policy, where student generation adds a new cost axis.
> others named
    uncertainty-aware feedback, agent-level distillation.

worth remembering:
> limits of this paper
    preprint, not peer reviewed yet. its critique targets xu et al. 2024 by
    name, not mansourian. as a survey whose whole point is the on/off split,
    it has a reason to stress how much earlier surveys missed it.
> link to my work
    the recipe in section 7, off-policy first then on-policy, is exactly
    what qwen3 did, and it can be shrunk to a small student for the
    experiment.


[qwen3_technical_report_2025]

scope:
> scope of the paper
    a full technical report on the qwen3 model family: architecture,
    pre-training on about 36 trillion tokens in 119 languages, a four-stage
    post-training pipeline and evaluation. distillation is only a small
    part: section 4.5, page 12, and the comparison in section 4.7.
    arXiv, may 2025, open weights under apache 2.0.
> scope for my work
    not a KD paper. it matters as the first big industrial proof that
    on-policy distillation works, and as the cleanest side-by-side of
    off-policy, RL and on-policy from the same starting point. useful for
    the "where the field is now" part of the talk, not for the core theory.

where it sits in KD terms:
> scheme
    offline. teachers qwen3-32B or qwen3-235B-A22B are fully trained
    first and stay fixed.
> source
    white-box, logit-based. the student matches the teacher's logits,
    which works because teacher and student are the same family and share a
    tokenizer.
> policy
    both, one after the other: off-policy first, then on-policy.
> family relation
    strong-to-weak within one model family. same architecture style, only
    the size changes.

section 4 intro is the post-training pipeline:
> flagship models
    the big models go through four stages: long chain-of-thought cold
    start, reasoning RL, thinking mode fusion, general RL. stages 1-2 build
    thinking ability, stages 3-4 add the non-thinking mode.
> lightweight models
    the small models skip that pipeline and are distilled from the
    flagships instead: dense 0.6B, 1.7B, 4B, 8B, 14B and the 30B-A3B MoE.
    note: figure 1 seems to list 30B-A3B as a flagship while section 4.5
    lists it as a student; the paper is not consistent here.
> why distill
    running four stages for every small model is expensive. distillation
    gives better scores, keeps the thinking / non-thinking control, and
    needs about 1/10 of the GPU hours.

section 4.5 is strong-to-weak distillation:
> phase 1, off-policy
    the student is trained on teacher outputs written in both thinking and
    non-thinking mode. this is the deepseek-R1 recipe. goal: basic reasoning
    and the ability to switch modes.
> phase 2, on-policy
    prompts are sampled, the student writes its own answer in either mode,
    and the student's logits are pulled toward the teacher's logits on
    that same text by minimizing KL divergence. this is the GKD / song &
    zheng recipe. which KL direction is used is not stated.

section 4.7 is the comparison, the key result:
> setup
    one 8B checkpoint after phase 1 is continued in two ways: with RL, or
    with on-policy distillation. math and code queries only.
> results
    AIME 2024: off-policy 55.0, plus RL 67.6, plus on-policy 74.4.
    AIME 2025: 42.8, 55.5, 65.5. MATH500: 92.4, 94.8, 97.0.
    LiveCodeBench v5: 42.0, 52.9, 60.3. GPQA-Diamond: 55.6, 61.3, 63.3.
    GPU hours: RL 17,920, on-policy 1,800, about 10x less.
> pass@64
    pass@64 means at least one of 64 tries is right. on AIME it goes up
    with on-policy distillation, 90.0 to 93.3 on 2024, but RL leaves it
    unchanged. the authors read this as distillation widening the student's
    exploration space, while RL only sharpens what the model can already
    do.

distilled small models vs baselines, tables 17-19:
> mostly better
    qwen3-4B in thinking mode beats deepseek-R1-distill-qwen-14B on AIME
    2024 and 2025, at less than a third of the size.
> not always
    qwen3-8B loses to R1-distill-qwen-32B on AIME 2024; qwen3-0.6B is well
    below R1-distill-qwen-1.5B. the authors' claim that small models beat
    larger baselines holds broadly, not everywhere.

worth remembering:
> limits of this paper
    a company report, not peer reviewed. the RL settings and how GPU hours
    were measured are not given, so the 10x number cannot be checked. the
    comparison covers math and code only.
> the bigger point
    qwen does not pick between off-policy and on-policy; it stacks them.
    off-policy builds the base cheaply, on-policy fixes what the student
    gets wrong on its own text.
> link to my work
    the same two-step recipe can be shrunk for the experiment: a small
    same-family student, off-policy SFT first, then a short on-policy phase,
    compared on a math benchmark.

[lu & thinking machines lab, 2025]

scope:
> who
    thinking machines lab is an AI company founded in 2025 by mira murati,
    former CTO of openai. its product tinker is an API for fine-tuning
    open models without running your own cluster.
> what this is
    a blog post, "on-policy distillation", kevin lu et al., october 2025.
    not a paper and not peer reviewed. it made the term popular.
> scope for my work
    the clearest intuition for on/off-policy, and a practical recipe. good
    for explaining the idea in the talk; numbers should be treated as a
    company demo.

main argument:
> the three-way table
    SFT: off-policy, dense signal. RL: on-policy, sparse signal, only a few
    bits per episode. on-policy distillation: on-policy and dense, the
    best of both.
> the chess picture
    like a chess engine grading every move you made in your own game,
    instead of only telling you at the end whether you won.

how it works:
> loss
    per-token reverse KL between student and teacher, computed on text the
    student sampled. mode-seeking: the student sticks to what the teacher
    thinks is likely.
> discount zero
    each token is graded only on itself, not on what comes after.
    longer-horizon versions did not help in their tests.
> cheap to add
    almost a one-line change to an RL script that already has a KL term:
    swap the reference model for the teacher. no reward model needed.

experiments:
> math reasoning
    student qwen3-8B-base, teacher qwen3-32B. after off-policy SFT on 400k
    prompts: 60% on AIME 2024. on-policy distillation on top reaches about
    70% in about 150 steps. reaching the same with more SFT would need
    about 2M prompts, estimated at roughly 9 to 30x more compute.
> qwen3 replication
    reproduces the qwen3 claim that on-policy distillation beats RL at a
    fraction of the cost, but got 70%, not qwen's 74.4%.
> personalization
    an assistant trained on internal company documents learns the
    knowledge but forgets how to follow instructions: IF-eval drops from
    85% to 45%. a short on-policy distillation phase from the original
    model brings it back to 83% while keeping the new knowledge.
> continual learning
    even SFT on the model's own outputs slowly breaks it. on-policy
    distillation from a fixed teacher does not, which makes it a candidate
    method for continual learning.

limits:
> stated by the authors
    simple demonstrations, not full studies. one model family only. the
    teacher's behavior must already be somewhat reachable by the student,
    otherwise SFT or mid-training is needed first. reverse KL cannot teach
    tokens the student almost never produces. cost numbers partly
    extrapolated.

worth remembering:
> the bigger point
    same message as qwen3: SFT first to give the student a base, then
    on-policy distillation to fix the student's own mistakes.
> link to my work
    the recipe is small enough to try at small scale, and the
    personalization case shows a use of distillation beyond compression:
    repairing a model after fine-tuning.
